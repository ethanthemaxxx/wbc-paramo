Páramo. Forecasting the
water of the Andes.

The water starts up there.
So do we.

Know how much water is coming.

Páramo reads the mountains, the satellites and the rain, then tells you what to expect in 7, 30 and 90 days.

Request access

Bogotá drinks from the mountains. Much of that water is gathered by páramos like Chingaza, slowly, in fog and moss.

We listen to those mountains with sensors, satellites and rain gauges. Then we turn what they say into a number you can plan around.

A city should know how much water it will have long before the tap asks.

A forecast is a chain of small, careful readings. Each one starts with a single .

01 · Sensors

Listen to the mountain

Small solar-powered stations stand among the frailejones. They record soil moisture, fog, air temperature and the level of the first streams. Each one reports a few times an hour, even when the road is closed and the clouds sit low. This is where every forecast begins.

02 · Watersheds

Follow the water downhill

Satellite images and rainfall records fill the gaps between stations. We trace where each drop will go, from the páramo into streams, then into reservoirs and rivers. The mountain becomes a set of watersheds, each with its own memory of past rain, dry spells and fog.

03 · Flows

Measure what reaches the stream

Next we estimate how much water moves through each stream, and how fast. Sponge-like soil releases it slowly, so the páramo’s recent past matters as much as today’s rain. We compare each flow with years of records and with what your own gauges already report.

04 · Forecast

Look 7, 30 and 90 days ahead

The model reads all of it together and returns three horizons: next week, next month and the next three months. Each forecast comes with a range, not a single promise, and a plain note on how sure we are. When the range is wide, we say so.

05 · Alerts

Warn the people who decide

You choose the thresholds that matter: a reservoir level, a dry stretch, a flood risk. When a forecast crosses one, an alert reaches your phone or inbox with the number, the date and the watershed. Then someone can open a valve, move a harvest or call a neighbor.
